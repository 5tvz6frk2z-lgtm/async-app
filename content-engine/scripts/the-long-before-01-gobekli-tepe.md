## COLD OPEN (0:00–0:25)

In 1994, a shepherd walking a dry hillside in southern Turkey notices a worked stone edge breaking through the soil. [VERIFY — popular discovery account; formal account: 1963 survey dismissed the site, Klaus Schmidt recognized it in 1994] [ART: golden-hour hillside, lone figure and flock, slow push-in] Archaeologists had surveyed this hill thirty years earlier. They recorded it as nothing much — a medieval cemetery, probably. [TEXT ON SCREEN: Göbekli Tepe — "Potbelly Hill"] They were wrong by more than eleven thousand years. Beneath the shepherd's feet stands the oldest monumental temple ever found — raised before farming, before pottery, before the wheel — by people who, according to everything we thought we knew, shouldn't have been able to build it. [MUSIC: swell, hard cut to silence] This is the site that broke the timeline of civilization.

## THE QUESTION (0:25–0:50)

One question, then. How did people with no metal, no wheels, and no farms raise rings of multi-ton carved stone on a hilltop — and why, after centuries of use, did they do the one thing no one can explain: make it all disappear? [ART: pillar circle from above, torch-lit, tiny figures] Answer that, and you don't just solve a site. You reorder the story of how civilization begins — which is exactly what this place has already started to do.

## THE PLACE & TIME (0:50–3:00)

Southeastern Turkey. The Germuş mountains look down on the plain of Harran, near the city of Şanlıurfa. [MAP: orbital push from space to the tell] The hill itself is artificial — a mound fifteen meters high and three hundred meters across [VERIFY — tell dimensions ~15m high, ~8 ha], built up from centuries of construction, burial, and rebuilding. Locals call it Göbekli Tepe. Potbelly Hill.

Now set your clock. The oldest layers here date to around 9500 BC — roughly eleven and a half thousand years ago. [VERIFY — PPNA dating, c. 9600–9500 BC] [TEXT ON SCREEN: c. 9500 BC] The last ice age has barely ended. There are no cities anywhere on Earth. No writing. No pottery. No bronze. No wheel. [SFX: soft pulse on each item] Stonehenge is further from these builders than Stonehenge is from you — this place is older by some six thousand years. [VERIFY — Stonehenge c. 3000 BC] And when workers raise the first pyramid at Giza, Göbekli Tepe has already been buried and forgotten for longer than the span between that pyramid and this video. [VERIFY — burial c. 8000 BC; Giza c. 2600 BC; gap ≈ 5,400 yrs vs 4,600 yrs to present]

The people who built it hunted gazelle and gathered wild grain. Textbooks said people like this lived in small bands, moved with the seasons, and built nothing that lasts. [ART: hunter band moving through tall wild grass, dawn] The textbooks were about to lose a chapter. And that's when the story stops making sense.

## THE EVIDENCE TOUR (3:00–6:30)

Walk the excavated enclosures. [ART: aerial of the dig, circular enclosures nested like rings of a tree] Stone circles, ten to thirty meters across [VERIFY — enclosure dimensions], walls curving around pairs of massive T-shaped limestone pillars. The tallest stand five and a half meters — a two-story building — and weigh several tons apiece. [VERIFY — pillar heights to ~5.5m; weights ~8–10 t claims] In the bedrock quarry a few hundred meters away lies an unfinished pillar nearly seven meters long. Estimated weight: around fifty tons. [VERIFY — quarry pillar dimensions/weight] [ART: the unfinished pillar still fused to bedrock, human figure for scale] [MUSIC: hold on silence, two beats]

Look closer at the stone. Foxes. Snakes. Scorpions. Boars. Vultures with spread wings. Carved in relief, with confidence — this is not the first thing these artists ever made. [ART: macro of pillar reliefs, raking torchlight] Some pillars have arms, hands, belts. They aren't columns. They're bodies. Stone beings, standing in rings, facing inward at something we can't see. [TEXT ON SCREEN: Pillar 43 — "the Vulture Stone"]

Now do the arithmetic the builders force on you. Quarrying, moving, and raising stones this size takes coordinated crews — by most estimates, hundreds of people at a time. [VERIFY — labor estimates] Hundreds of hunter-gatherers, in one place, fed for weeks. And the ground remembers the feeding: massive deposits of gazelle bone [VERIFY — faunal assemblage], and limestone basins that could hold a hundred and sixty liters, bearing chemical traces some researchers read as the residue of brewed grain. [VERIFY — oxalate/brewing evidence, contested] Possibly the oldest beer in the world — poured at the oldest temple in the world. [ART: firelit feast among the pillars, night]

So: no metal tools — flint. No wheels — sledges, ropes, muscle. No draft animals — none were domesticated yet. [VERIFY] Three impossibilities, stacked. And the third one hides the real shock: whoever organized this could summon and command labor on a scale the textbooks reserved for kings — six thousand years before kings. Which raises the obvious question. Who was this all *for*?

Hold that. Because the site has one more move.

## RE-HOOK (~5:45)

[MUSIC: cut] And then there's the strangest choice of all. Around ten thousand years ago, the enclosures were filled in — thousands of tons of rubble, bone, and soil, packed around the pillars until the rings vanished into the hill. [VERIFY — backfilling c. 8000 BC; degree of deliberateness is actively debated] [ART: pillars half-swallowed by earth, figures with baskets, dawn light] Not abandoned. *Filled.* And whether that burial was ritual, practical, or partly the hill itself sliding back over the stones — the effect was the same. The greatest monument on Earth… disappeared from it. People protect what they build. What makes a civilization entomb its greatest work?

## THE THEORIES (6:15–9:30)

Start where the evidence is strongest. The excavator who gave his life to this site, Klaus Schmidt, read it as a sanctuary — a ritual gathering place for scattered bands, a cathedral before there were congregations. His phrase became famous: first came the temple, then the city. [VERIFY — Schmidt quote] [ART: converging bands of travelers crossing the plain toward the hill] And that idea carries a heresy inside it. Feeding ritual gatherings of hundreds takes more wild grain than gathering easily supplies. Some researchers think that pressure — feed the festival — is what pushed people toward planting, harvesting, staying. Follow that logic and farming didn't build the temple. The temple built farming. Civilization begins not with a full stomach, but with an idea. [MUSIC: single sustained note, then silence]

Now the honest complication — because this channel doesn't sell you certainty. Newer excavation has found rainwater channels, cisterns, and structures that look suspiciously like houses. [VERIFY — later findings: water management, domestic traces] The "temple with no town" may have had a town after all — and the burial layers may be part slopewash, part human backfill rather than one grand ritual act. [VERIFY] The site is still rewriting itself. That's not a weakness in the story. That's what a live excavation looks like.

And then there are the other claims — you've heard them. That a site this old, this sophisticated, must be the fingerprint of a lost advanced civilization, survivors of a cataclysm at the end of the ice age. It's a seductive story. Here's what the ground says. The tools in every layer are flint. The quarry, the unfinished pillar, the flint chips by the hundreds of thousands — the whole production line is right there on the hill, and every link in it is Stone Age. [VERIFY — lithic assemblage] The dating is boringly consistent. Nothing anomalous, nothing out of place. The evidence doesn't point to a forgotten supercivilization. It points to something stranger: people exactly like us, with nothing but stone, rope, and belief — doing this. [ART: wide shot, ring of pillars under the Milky Way]

## THE LADDER (9:30–11:30)

So — the ladder. [TEXT ON SCREEN: THE LADDER] [SFX: ladder cue]

WE KNOW — the stones are real, the dates are real: hunter-gatherers raised multi-ton carved pillars here around eleven and a half thousand years ago, and the enclosures were later filled and hidden. [VERIFY — as above]

RESEARCHERS THINK — this was a ritual center, possibly with a resident community; and the demands of gathering here may have helped push humanity toward agriculture itself. The temple before the town — maybe the temple *making* the town.

SOME SPECULATE — lost civilizations, forgotten sciences. The tool marks, the quarry, and the dating all say otherwise.

And what would settle it? Digging. Ground-penetrating surveys suggest many more enclosures still inside the hill — by most estimates, the great majority of the site has never been excavated. [VERIFY — geophysics; "~90–95% unexcavated" claims] Meanwhile, just across these hills, sister sites — Karahan Tepe and the wider Taş Tepeler project — are already producing pillars, carvings, and buildings of the same deep age. [VERIFY — Taş Tepeler program status] This isn't one anomaly. It's a lost world, surfacing one hilltop at a time. [MAP: region, sister sites lighting up one by one]

Eleven and a half thousand years ago, people with no writing left us a message anyway — in the only permanent ink they had.
