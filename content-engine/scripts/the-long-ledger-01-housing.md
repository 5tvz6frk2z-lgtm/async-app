## ACT 1 — HOW IT'S SUPPOSED TO WORK (0:45–3:00)

Rewind to 1930. You want to buy a house. The bank wants half the price up front. In cash. [B-ROLL: Depression-era bank lobby, archival] The loan they give you on the rest runs three to five years — then the whole balance comes due at once, and you refinance or you lose the house. [VERIFY — pre-New Deal mortgage terms: ~50% down, 3–5 year balloon] [TEXT ON SCREEN: 50% down. 5 years. Good luck.] When the Depression hits, that system detonates. Millions can't refinance. Foreclosures sweep the country. [SFX: single low thud, beat of silence]

So the government invents something that sounds boring and changes everything: the thirty-year, fixed-rate, low-down-payment mortgage. [VERIFY — FHA founding 1934; standardization of long-term amortized loans] [TEXT ON SCREEN: 1934 — the 30-year mortgage] Thirty years to pay. The rate never moves. Suddenly a house isn't a fortune — it's a monthly payment.

Now add the second part of the machine: supply. After the war, builders industrialize the house itself. At Levittown, crews frame a new home every sixteen minutes. [VERIFY — Levittown production rate; ~$8,000 price ≈ roughly 2x a factory wage of the era] [B-ROLL: archival Levittown assembly-line construction] Cheap land, mass production, long cheap credit. That's the whole machine. Three parts. And for a generation it runs so well that a single income buys a house, and the ratio holds near two and a half. [CHART: ratio line, flat and calm through the 1950s–60s]

Then, one part at a time, the machine stops. That's the what. Here's the why.

## ACT 2 — WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENS (3:00–5:30)

Part one broke: land. Over decades, American cities zoned most of their residential ground so that only one thing can legally be built on it — a single detached house. In many cities, that's roughly three-quarters of all residential land. [VERIFY — single-family zoning share, e.g., ~75% figure by metro] [CHART: city map, single-family zones flooding gold] Not apartments. Not townhomes. Not the small cheap starter homes Levittown mass-produced. One house, one lot, by law. Supply didn't fall. It was capped.

Part two broke: builders. In 2005, America starts construction on about two million homes. By 2009, five hundred and fifty thousand. [VERIFY — Census housing starts: ~2.07M in 2005, ~554K in 2009] [CHART: housing starts cliff] The crash doesn't just pause construction — it destroys the construction industry. Builders fold. Crews scatter into other trades. And for a full decade, the country builds far fewer homes than it forms households. Add up the missing years and estimates put the hole at close to four million homes. [VERIFY — Freddie Mac ~3.8M shortage estimate; ranges to 6M+] That's roughly every home in the state of Ohio — missing. [VERIFY comparison math] [TEXT ON SCREEN: ~4,000,000 missing homes]

Part three: demand never got the memo. The largest generation in American history — millennials — aged straight into family-forming years while the hole was still open. [CHART: households formed vs. homes completed, lines diverging]

Remember two and a half? By 2019, the ratio had already climbed past four. [VERIFY] [CHART: ratio line bending upward] And then 2020 happened.

## ACT 3 — FOLLOW THE MONEY (5:45–9:00)

Follow the money. When houses are scarce, three groups get paid.

Group one: investors. Rock-bottom rates turned the family home into an asset class — a rent check with a roof on it. Here's the honest version, because the internet loves to exaggerate this: nationally, the giant institutions own a small share of single-family homes. [VERIFY — institutional ownership ~low single digits of SFH stock] But averages hide the mechanism. Investor buying concentrates in exactly the affordable metros where first-time buyers shop — and in those places, at the peak, roughly one purchase in four was an investor. [VERIFY — metro-level investor purchase shares, e.g., Atlanta/Phoenix] [CHART: investor share by metro, Sun Belt glowing] Prices are set at the margin. You don't need to own every house to move the price of the next one. You just need to be the highest bidder often enough.

Group two: everyone who already owns. Here's the part polite conversation skips. Every existing homeowner is paid by the shortage — on paper, every month. [CHART: homeowner equity total rising] And homeowners vote. When a new apartment building comes up at the zoning meeting, the people in the room are the people whose net worth depends on scarcity. Nobody has to be a villain. The incentive votes for them. [B-ROLL: empty city-council chamber, slow push]

Group three is the strangest: nobody — a lock with no locksmith. During the pandemic, millions of families refinanced near three percent. Then rates doubled. [VERIFY — share of mortgages below 4% at peak, ~60%] Selling your house now means trading a three percent loan for a seven percent one on the next place. So owners who would normally move... don't. Listings starve. [CHART: existing-home inventory at record lows] The market froze from the inside — and scarce listings meet the same crowd of buyers, so prices hold even as sales collapse. [TEXT ON SCREEN: The Rate Lock]

Put the three together: capped land, a missing four million homes, investor demand at the margin, and owners who can't afford to sell. From 2020 to 2022, the median American home price rose over forty percent. [VERIFY — Case-Shiller / NAR 2020–2022] [CHART: the ratio line going vertical, ending at ~6] Not a bubble of optimism. A machine, running in reverse.

## THE LEDGER (9:00–11:00)

Here's the ledger. [MUSIC: cut to silence] [CHART: THE LEDGER — three numbers on navy, gold rules]

Number one: months of inventory. How long would it take to sell every home on the market at the current pace? Under four months, sellers hold all the power. Over six, buyers do. [VERIFY — NAR balanced-market convention] You can find it for your metro in one search.

Number two: price-to-income. The whole story of this video in one division problem. Under three: your grandparents' market. Past five: the median family can no longer buy the median home. [VERIFY thresholds against historical distribution]

Number three — the one nobody talks about: rent-versus-buy. Take the full monthly cost of owning the house — payment, taxes, insurance, upkeep — and divide it by the rent on the same house. Through most of history those track each other. When owning costs thirty, forty, fifty percent more than renting the identical home [VERIFY — 2023–2025 own-vs-rent premium estimates], the market is quietly telling you the price is wrong — because the difference isn't shelter, it's speculation. Rent is the truth serum of housing. [TEXT ON SCREEN: Rent doesn't lie.]

Framed as information, not advice: those three numbers, in your zip code, will tell you more than any national headline ever will. Where they go, prices follow.

The machine that built the middle class wasn't stolen in a night. It was switched off one part at a time — land, builders, credit — until houses stopped being places to live and became assets to hold. The math broke slowly, then all at once. And because the machine is policy, not physics, it can be rebuilt the same way it was broken: on purpose. [B-ROLL: framing crew raising a wall at sunrise] Watch the ledger, not the headlines.
